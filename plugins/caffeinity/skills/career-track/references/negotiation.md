# Negotiation: the raise, the promotion ask, the offer

Inherits the rules in `SKILL.md`. The one that bites hardest here: no invented figures, no invented offers, no invented market data. A number you cannot say where it came from does not go in the script, because it gets checked.

---

## Before you name a number

Work these out first. Each one changes what the ask should be, and each one is a place where a confident guess costs money.

| To establish | Why it decides the ask | If unknown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who can actually approve it | Your manager may recommend and not decide; the ask goes to the person with the budget | Ask your manager directly who signs it, before scripting |
| The cycle date and its rules | A raise requested two weeks before calibration is a different conversation from one requested in February | Get the date; do not assume a review meeting is the comp meeting |
| Whether the level change is agreed | Level first, then pay. Money at the old level is a different conversation from money at the new one | Say which you are asking for; the script splits if they are conflated |
| Your evidence case | The promotion narrative in `planning.md` is what makes the ask reasonable | No narrative, no ask: build the case first |
| Your floor and its reason | The number you can accept without resenting it. Set by your own costs and options | Work it out before the meeting, in private, written down |
| What else is on the table | Notice terms, title, level, bonus, equity, hours, remote days, training budget, review date | List them; the ask is a package, not one figure |
| Any competing offer that exists | Changes leverage and timing | If none exists, none is implied. Never suggest one that does not exist |
| Market data | Only useful if it is dated, sourced and comparable to your role, level and region | No source this session, no figure in the script. Reason from internal evidence instead |

Asking for these is not stalling. A single message that names what you need before the conversation is normal, and it is cheaper than a bad number said out loud.

## Reasoning about ranges

Three ways to arrive at a figure. State which you used, because the reader can tell when you have not thought it through.

1. **Relative to your current total.** A percentage move on what you earn now. Simple, defensible, and it inherits whatever is already wrong with your pay. Say what the number does to your total, not only to the base: bonus, equity refresh, pension or contribution, and the fixed items that do not move.
2. **Relative to the level.** The argument is that you are already doing the work at the next level, so the pay should follow the level. It leans entirely on the promotion narrative and on the level bar being real for this employer.
3. **Relative to an external market.** Only as far as the data goes. A published range needs: the source, the date you read it, the region, the role and level it actually covers, and whether it reports base or total. A salary survey covering a different market or a different mix of levels is not evidence about your employer.

**How to present a range and stay honest:**

- Label it: "estimate from <source>, read <date>, for <role/level/region>". No source read this session means no number in front of them.
- Never present a market estimate as the employer's band. If the band is unknown, the honest line is "I do not know your band for this level".
- Prefer a stated target with a floor behind it over a wide range. If you give a range, the top of it is what you actually want and the bottom is what you would decline, so a range is where most people lose.
- Reason in relative terms when absolute terms are unavailable: the gap between your evidence and the level you are asking for, the market movement in your function over the period, the cost of replacing you versus the cost of the raise. Where the third is used, be truthful about whether the employer can afford to lose you; if the honest answer is that they can, do not play it as leverage.
- Do not quote a peer's pay you were not told, and do not build an ask on a rumour about what someone else earns.
- If you have no source at all, run the conversation as an evidence-and-level ask and let the employer name a figure first. Ask what the range for the level is and what they think is fair, then respond to the number rather than generating one.

## The script skeleton

Six beats. Keep it short enough to say out loud without reading it. Anything longer than about two minutes of talking before you stop is too long.

```text
1. Frame       "I want to talk about the [role/level] I am doing and what it should pay."
2. Evidence    Two or three items from the promotion narrative. Facts, no adjectives.
               Each item: what I owned, what changed. [metric — supply this] where unknown.
3. Ask         The figure or the level, named once, plainly. <target>
4. Stop        Say nothing until they respond. The pause is theirs.
5. Respond     Take the answer and work with it. See the responses table.
6. Close       Who does what by when, in one sentence. Then write it down and send it.
```

Rules for the beats:

- **Frame** states the subject; it does not apologise for raising it, and it does not open with a threat.
- **Evidence** is the case, not a list of duties. Nobody got a raise for being busy.
- **Ask** gets said once. Repeating a number softer because the room went quiet is how a target becomes a wish.
- **Stop** is the hardest beat and the one most often skipped.
- **Close** always names a date and a person. "We will see" with no date is a no delivered politely.

Tone: cooperative, specific, unhurried. The ask is reasonable because the evidence is, not because the wording was clever.

## Responses and what they cost

| They say | What it usually means | Your move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yes | Ask what it looks like, at what date, at what level, in writing | Confirm the number, the effective date and the approver before the meeting ends |
| No, not now | Often "not without a case above me" | Ask what would change it and by when; get the criteria, then set a date to return |
| There is no budget | A constraint or a shield | Ask what is available outside the cycle, and what else moves: title, level, review date, training, equity, days |
| A lower number than the ask | Their opening position | Do not accept in the room. Ask what the number is based on, then say you will come back to it |
| "You are doing great, why do you want more?" | A genuine question or a deflection | Answer it once, flatly, with the evidence, then return to the ask |
| A promotion without the pay | A real outcome with a cost | Decide beforehand whether the level alone is worth it; if it is not, say what would make it worth it |
| Silence or a subject change | They are not ready | Name it: "I would like an answer by <date>, or the reason I cannot have one yet" |

Write down, before the meeting, which of these you have already decided an answer to. The ones you have not are where the deal gets made in the room on your behalf.

## The written ask

Use a short message when the conversation is formal, when it has to go up the chain, or when you need a record. Structure: subject line naming the topic, one line of context, the evidence in bullets, the ask in one sentence, the date by which you would like an answer, closing. Keep it under about twenty lines.

- Bullets are the evidence, not a biography. Each one a thing you owned and what changed.
- The ask appears once, in one sentence.
- Any figure carries its basis in the same breath, or the figure comes from them, not you.
- Never put an ultimatum in writing unless the alternative is one you would genuinely carry out.
- Send it and stop. Follow-up chasers are for the date you named, not before it.

## Lines that do not move

- No invented competing offers, no invented numbers, no inflated scope to make the ask look proportionate.
- No claims about what a named employer pays at a level unless it was sourced this session, and no presenting a market estimate as that employer's band.
- No promises about the outcome. This raises the quality of the ask; the decision is not yours or this skill's.
- No advice to burn the relationship to make a point. When the honest read is that the answer is never going to be yes where you are, name that and take it to the pivot path in `references/planning.md`.
- Notice periods, non-competes, contractual bonus wording, deferred equity and any question with a tribunal in it are not for this skill. One line: get it checked by someone who can advise on the contract.
