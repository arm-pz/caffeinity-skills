# Planning: positioning, the case, the review, the pivot, the network, the room

Every section inherits the rules in `SKILL.md`: numbers come from what you state, missing figures stay as `[metric — supply this]` slots, no outcome is promised, and nothing about a named employer is presented as fact without a source you read this session.

Shapes below use brackets where a real figure or fact must go. Never copy a bracketed placeholder or an example phrase into your document as though it were yours.

## Positioning and visibility

**Goal:** Make one thing about your work easy for the right people to see and to say to someone else.

**Inputs:** function, level, the work you actually do, the audience whose opinion moves the target (manager, skip-level, peers in other teams, an internal community, an external one).

**Method:**

1. Write the line. Subject is you, verb is a real activity, object is a result you can point at. Test: does a piece of work you did in the last quarter prove it? If not, it is a wish, not a positioning line.
2. Choose the venues. Two or three, not a list. Internal: architecture reviews, the team demo, a written post-mortem, a hiring panel, an internal guild. External: a talk, a written piece, an open-source contribution, a standards group. Each venue gets a line on who reads or hears it.
3. Set the cadence. One concrete contribution per venue per period, with the period named. A cadence you cannot keep in a bad month is not a cadence.
4. Name the risk of each venue being invisible: a thing done quietly that nobody outside your team learns about is a gap in the plan, not a failure of humility.

**Output:**

| Element | Content |
| --- | --- |
| The line | One sentence, evidenced by a named piece of work |
| Audience | Whose mental model needs to change, by role |
| Venues | Two or three, with what each one is for |
| Cadence | What you contribute, how often, next date |
| Proof of receipt | How you will know it landed, not how you hope it will |

**Rules:** the plan raises visibility, it does not confer standing. No claiming expertise in an area without evidence, no listing a role you have not held, no advice to buy or manufacture followers, no "post daily" filler that buries the work. If the honest read is that the line is not yet earned, say so and route the gap to study-system.

## Promotion narrative

**Goal:** Assemble the case for the next level, then the conversation that opens it.

**Inputs:** the level bar as you report it (a documented ladder, a rubric, a description a manager has given you), your evidence, who decides, the cycle date if known.

**Method:**

1. Get the bar in writing. If the ladder is not written down, the honest statement is "the bar is undocumented; here is what my manager has said", and the plan's first action is to ask for the criteria in writing.
2. Map evidence to bar, one row per criterion. A row with no evidence is a gap, not something to blur.
3. Write in scope, not adjectives. The pattern is: the situation, what you owned, what you did, what changed afterwards, and who else could do it. Adjectives ("high-impact", "strategic") get deleted; the clause that proves them stays.
4. State at the level you are aiming at without describing duties you have not performed. Writing the sentence as though you ran a team you do not run is inflation and it collapses in the meeting.
5. Name the counter-case: the two strongest reasons a sceptic gives for not yet, and the honest answer to each. A case with no counter-case is a draft.

**Output:**

| Bar item (as reported) | Your evidence | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <criterion> | <what you did and what changed> | Met / Partial / Gap |

Then: a short narrative under each criterion, the counter-case, the ask, and the conversation script (who, opening line, what you request, what you do if the answer is "not yet" and what a date-bound follow-up looks like).

**Rules:** no percentages, team sizes or budgets that you have not supplied. Do not assert what your company's calibration meeting does. Do not predict a decision or a date.

## Performance-review self-assessment

**Goal:** Write the self-assessment in your employer's shape, from facts, strong enough that a promotion ask follows from it rather than being bolted on.

**Inputs:** the section headings or competencies you are asked to write against, your evidence base, anything you want in the review that is not in the evidence (feedback quotes, delivery dates, a stakeholder's words).

**Method:**

1. One section per competency the form names. Do not invent competencies your form does not have, and do not merge two because one is thin; a thin one gets an honest gap sentence.
2. Per competency: what you owned, what you did, what changed, what you would do differently. The last part is the one that reads as senior; leaving it out looks evasive.
3. Quantify only what you have. Where a figure would land and you do not have it, write the scope in words you can defend and leave the slot: "cut the release process for the billing service; time saved per release: [metric — supply this]".
4. Pull the promotion case in where the form invites forward-looking answers, and keep it out where it does not. A request for more money buried in a free-text box about teamwork gets read as a mistake.
5. Keep the register flat. Short declarative sentences about specific things. Superlatives invite the reader to check, and the check fails.

**Output:** the drafted sections in the form's order, then a short list of **Slots to close** with one question per slot, then **Not claimed**, the lines you deliberately left qualitative because the number is not available.

**Rules:** never state a result you cannot say out loud in the meeting. Do not import another company's competency names. Do not write the manager's summary for them.

## Pivot path

**Goal:** Get from the current function to the target one with the least wasted movement.

**Inputs:** current role and tenure, target role, why the move, what you can do but have not been paid to do, money and time constraints, whether the move can happen internally.

**Method:**

1. Build the transferable-skill map: three columns, the skill, where you demonstrated it in the current role, what it is called in the target role. Naming is most of the work in a pivot; the same activity has different vocabulary per function.
2. Find the bridge roles. The target is rarely adjacent. List the roles that sit between current and target and that you could reach without a retrain, then pick the shortest hop.
3. List the proof you still need: artefacts, a scope you have run, a qualification, a portfolio piece. Sort by how long each takes.
4. Order the friction points: pay step down, title loss, seniority reset, relocation, the credential gap, the network gap. Say which you can absorb and which you cannot.
5. Check whether the move can be made where you are: a secondment, an internal transfer, a stretch assignment inside the current team. It is usually the cheapest route and the first one to test.

**Output:** the map table, bridge roles with why each is reachable, proof list with time estimates labelled as estimates, friction list with your tolerance, and the first action for each of the two or three routes.

**Rules:** do not assert a role's salary or a market's demand without a dated source. Do not tell someone to quit in order to study when the cost was never discussed. Time estimates carry the assumption they rest on.

## Network map

**Goal:** Get in front of the people who know about work before it is posted, without treating every contact as a transaction.

**Inputs:** target role or function, region or remote constraint, who you already know, how much time you realistically have per week.

**Method:**

1. Map by role, not name. You do not know names; the skill does not invent them. Write the roles that matter: a person doing the target job, a hiring manager for it, someone who made this pivot in the last two years, an internal talent or resourcing contact, a former colleague now somewhere relevant.
2. For each, the reason it matters and the ask. The ask is small, specific and not "a job": a question about their day, a read on one piece of work, an introduction to one person.
3. Sort contacts into: close, loose, and not yet in contact. Each gets a different opening. A cold message and a message to someone you worked with three years ago are different texts, and the second one that pretends to be the first reads badly.
4. Cadence with dates. A small number of conversations you actually hold beats a long list. Name the next date and the follow-up interval.
5. Log what you learn back into the plan: the vocabulary people use for the work, the skills that come up unprompted, the teams that hire.

**Output:**

| Role to reach | Why | The ask | State | Next date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Plus message drafts per state, and a line on where each conversation feeds the plan.

**Rules:** no invented names, companies or contact details. No advice to misrepresent a relationship, mass-message, or scrape an address book. Do not promise that a conversation leads to an opportunity. Where the search itself is what you want, tracked and chased, that is application-pilot's work and the network map goes to it as input.

## Executive presence

**Goal:** Change what senior people infer from your behaviour in the room, in the situations you describe.

**Inputs:** the specific situations where it is going wrong (a standing update, a review with a hostile question, a room where you are the only one from your team, a written escalation), what you currently do there, what you would like to be different, whose read of you matters.

**Method:**

1. Start from the situation, not the trait. "Be more confident" is not workable. "In the monthly ops review, you answer the first question you were asked and then stop" is.
2. Infer what a senior reader is likely taking from that behaviour, phrased as a reading and not as a fact about them: "the common read of stopping after one answer is that you do not know the second-order effects".
3. Give a behaviour change per situation, small enough to run next occurrence: who you address, when you stop speaking, what you ask for, what you say when you do not know, how you handle being interrupted.
4. Cover the written room where it is the problem: escalation length, leading with the decision needed, naming the risk rather than burying it.
5. Name what to drop: hedging openers, apologising for taking the slot, answering a question that was not asked, volunteering for work nobody assigned you as a way of becoming visible.

**Output:**

| Situation | What you do now | Likely read | Change to run | How you will know it worked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Rules: no personality diagnosis, no advice about voice, appearance, accent or mannerisms tied to a protected characteristic, no claim about how a named executive thinks. Style coaching that touches those areas is out of scope and said in one line.
