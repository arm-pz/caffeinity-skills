# Sourcing, attribution and claims

Read this when the text cites sources, quotes anyone, or makes factual claims about people, companies or events. It does not apply to an email, a personal note or copy with no claims to support.

## Attribution

Name the source and say what it says. Do not write "experts say", "observers have noted", "critics argue", "industry reports suggest", "sources indicate" or "many believe" when a specific source or person can be identified.

Do not imply that several sources support something when one or two do.

Summarise what a source says. "Covered", "featured", "profiled" and "recognised" report that a source exists and leave out its content.

Leave out the quality, independence, prestige or media category of a source unless that is relevant. Leave out that a person or organisation has an "active social media presence" unless that fact matters.

## Facts and citations

Never fabricate a citation, URL, quotation, DOI, ISBN, page number, publication, author or source. Do not produce a link that looks plausible and does not exist.

When verification is required, cite only sources you have checked, and check that the source supports the claim attached to it. That a source exists is not evidence for a claim.

Every reference listed must be used in the text. No reference may contain an unfinished placeholder.

For a specific claim from a book or long document, give the page or location when it is available and useful.

Remove tracking parameters from URLs when they are not needed.

Never leave internal citation syntax, search IDs, content-reference IDs, model markers, attachment tags or other system artifacts in finished writing.

## What is known and what is missing

Keep verified facts, reasonable inference, speculation and opinion distinct, and say which is which.

If information is missing, say exactly what is missing. Do not speculate about what it "likely" says, and do not invent details to complete a pattern.

Do not use disclaimers such as "limited", "scarce", "not widely documented" or "based on available information" as filler. Do not mention a training cutoff unless it bears on the answer.

## Contested material

Do not smooth conflicting evidence into one confident account.

Attribute contested claims. The framing used by a government, corporation, activist group or critic belongs to that party, so do not repeat it as neutral fact.

Apply the same standard of evidence whether or not a source or subject fits a familiar story.
