# Memo format

One document, seven blocks, in this order. Every claim carries a label: `[supplied]` the user said it, `[assumed]` the skill needed it to continue, `[unknown]` nobody has it. A block that would need proof to be written ships as a framework with a slot, not as a filled paragraph.

## The document

```
# <Business> — <the decision>, <date>
Decider: <who signs off>    Deadline: <when>    Version: <n>

## 0. The decision requested
One line. What is being decided, by whom, by when. If the user's question is not
answerable in this form, that is the first finding.

## 1. Situation analysis
What is true now, in the user's business and market. Only the load-bearing facts,
each labelled, each traceable to what the user said or a dated source read in the
session. Sub-sections as needed: position, customers, competitors, model, numbers.

## 2. Recommendation
The pick, in the first sentence, in the affirmative, with a date on it. Then:
- Why this over the alternatives, named.
- The ranked options table it came out of.
- The tradeoff accepted, stated as a cost rather than a footnote.
- Confidence: high, medium or low, plus the one thing that would raise it.
- What would change my mind: the specific number or observation.

## 3. Execution roadmap
The ninety-day plan in weeks, with owners and gates. Then the one-year and
three-year horizons as milestones, owners and success metrics. Ninety-day is a
plan; the further horizons are plans plus the bets they rest on, marked as such.

## 4. Assumptions and open questions
Every `[assumed]` line from the document, gathered here, each with what it would
take to check it. Every open question the memo cannot answer. If the user said
"go anyway", this block holds the assumptions used, in the order they were made.

## 5. Dissent
The strongest argument against section 2, written as its best supporter would
put it. Then the answer to it, or the admission that it lands and the
recommendation is conditional on that point.

## 6. Gaps
Every slot in the document, one line each: what is missing, which skill or which
person normally fills it, and what it blocks. Slots left out of the prose stay
here so nothing quietly becomes a fact later.
```

Section 5 exists because a memo without it is advocacy. Keep it to a short paragraph; do not turn it into a full attack, that is `crash-test`'s job.

## Length

A decision memo runs two to four pages of text. A single-mode ask, pricing or a SWOT, is one of these blocks plus the table, and does not need the rest. If the memo goes past four pages, the situation analysis is repeating itself; cut it rather than adding appendices.

## Board presentation, as a text outline

Structure and argument only. No slide graphics, no invented agenda for a named company, no claims about what a board typically wants unless the user said so. Order the pages so the decision is on page two; an executive who has to reach the end to know what is being asked does not approve.

```
1. Title            business, decision, date, version
2. The ask          what approval is being requested, and the cost of not deciding
3. Situation        three lines, each labelled; no new facts after this page
4. Evidence         the numbers the decision rests on, with source and date
5. Options          the ranked table, one line per option, tradeoff visible
6. Recommendation   the pick, the confidence, what would change it
7. Plan             ninety-day in weeks; the one-year and three-year milestones
8. Cost and risk    what this spends, what it exposes, the reversibility
9. Ask again        the same decision, restated, with the date it is needed by
10. Appendix        assumptions block, dissent line, gaps, sourcing detail
```

Per page, write: the page title, the one sentence it proves, and the label of the evidence behind it. If page 4 has no dated source and nothing supplied, the page says "no evidence yet, and here is what would settle it".

Speaker notes are one line per page: what you want the room to do with it.

## One-page version

When the user needs it fast, the whole memo collapses to:

```
Decision: <one line, by whom, by when>
Situation: <three labelled lines>
Recommendation: <the pick, the tradeoff accepted, confidence>
Next ninety days: <the milestones with owners>
Assumptions: <the ones that would break this>
Dissent: <the strongest argument against>
Gaps: <what is missing, and what it blocks>
```

Never drop Dissent or Gaps to make it fit. Drop Situation detail and point to the fuller memo.

## Naming and phrasing rules

- Numbers in the prose are quoted from the tables, never rounded into vagueness or inflated.
- Write "expected", "if this holds", "we have no reading on this". Do not write "will", "guaranteed", "proven" about a future outcome.
- Any statement about a platform's or a competitor's behaviour is labelled with its source or marked `[unverified]`.
- The recommendation section never cites a framework as if it were evidence. A canvas is a way of writing down what the user said.
- Owners are named roles. "The team" owns nothing and no line with that as an owner passes the check list.
